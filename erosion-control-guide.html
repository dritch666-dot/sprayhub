⛰️ Erosion Control on a Slope with Australian Native Plants

Bare, sloping soil is a problem waiting for the next big storm. The fix is rarely one thing — it's a sequence: control the water, prepare the soil, protect the surface, and get living native cover established fast. This guide walks the whole job, from reading the slope to choosing the right natives for your climate zone and keeping them alive through the first summer.

🌧️ Manage water first 🧵 Match matting to steepness 🌱 Layer groundcover → grass → shrub → tree 🎯 Aim for 70%+ cover

The core idea

You are not just "planting a slope". You're doing three jobs at once: slowing and diverting water, armouring the soil surface (mulch, matting, cover), and reinforcing the soil with roots. Do only one and it usually fails. The five steps in this guide — assess, prepare, choose plants, plant, and establish — put them in the right order.

💡 Who this is for

Landholders, gardeners, agronomists and contractors stabilising an eroding batter, cutting, creek bank or garden slope with Australian natives — anywhere in the country.

Why slopes erode

Erosion is simply soil moving faster than it's being held. On a slope, gravity, water and bare ground work together — and the steeper and longer the slope, the more energy the runoff carries. The forms of water erosion escalate in stages:

  • Splash / raindrop erosion — raindrops hit bare soil, detach particles and seal the surface, cutting infiltration.
  • Sheet erosion — a thin, even layer of topsoil is peeled off across the whole slope; easy to miss until the topsoil is gone.
  • Rill erosion — runoff concentrates into small channels you could step over; the sign water is gaining power.
  • Gully erosion — rills deepen into channels too big to drive across or rake away; expensive to repair.
  • Tunnel erosion — water moves through dispersive (sodic) subsoil and collapses into gullies later (see Step 2).

Four factors decide how bad it gets: rainfall intensity, slope gradient and length, soil erodibility (dispersive/sodic and silty soils are worst), and ground cover — the factor you control most. Erosion drops sharply once living and dead cover exceeds roughly 70%.

How plants actually control erosion

Vegetation isn't decoration here — each layer does mechanical work.

Canopy

Intercepts rain so drops hit the ground with far less energy.

Groundcover + litter

Armours the surface, takes the raindrop hit and slows surface flow so water soaks in.

Roots

Physically bind soil particles and add shear strength — the "rebar" of the slope.

Whole plant

Transpiration and better infiltration lower soil moisture and reduce runoff.

🧪 Why strappy natives and native grasses are erosion superstars

Dense fibrous root systems bind far more soil than a single taproot. Field trials on strappy natives such as Lomandra and Dianella have measured very large increases in soil shear strength versus bare soil. Combine fibrous binders (grasses, strappy plants) with deeper-rooted shrubs and trees for anchoring, and prostrate groundcovers for instant surface protection.

Step 1 — Assess the site

Ten minutes of assessment saves a season of failed plants. Slope is described three ways; matting ratings use the ratio (horizontal : vertical — "3:1" means 3 m across for every 1 m of drop):

Ratio (H:V)PercentDegreesCharacter
3:1~33%~18°Gentle — easy to plant
2:1~50%~27°Steep — matting territory
1.5:1~67%~34°Very steep
1:1100%45°Extreme — engineering + heavy coir

Then check five things

  • Soil type & dispersion — test for dispersive (sodic) soil: drop an air-dry crumb into a glass of clear water; a milky halo means it's dispersive and needs gypsum + fast cover.
  • Where the water comes from — walk uphill; concentrated runoff arriving from above will destroy new plantings unless intercepted.
  • Aspect & exposure — hot north/west faces dry fast (choose tough species); wind and salt narrow the list.
  • Existing vegetation — keep every established plant you can; its roots already hold the slope.
  • Access & safety — anything steeper than ~2:1 is hazardous; plan access and never work directly below loosened soil.

Step 2 — Prepare the site

Preparation is where erosion jobs are won or lost. Sequence: water, shape, soil, weeds, armour.

a. Control the water first

Before anything else, divert runoff arriving from above the slope with a contour bank, catch drain or diversion drain across the top, leading to a stable, vegetated outlet. Never let concentrated flow spill onto a slope you're trying to establish.

b. Reshape to a stable angle

The most effective structural fix is to batter the slope back to a gentler gradient — aim for 2:1 or flatter, ideally 3:1. On tall or space-limited slopes, cut benches or terraces to break the slope length (shorter slopes = less runoff energy), each draining to a safe outlet.

🚨 Dispersive / sodic soils — handle with care

Do not expose fresh dispersive subsoil and leave it bare — the classic trigger for rilling and tunnel erosion. Ameliorate with gypsum (commonly ~0.5–1 kg/m², or per soil test) worked into the surface, add organic matter, and cover with mulch/matting and plants fast. Existing tunnels usually need ripping out, treating and recompacting — not just filling.

c. Loosen the surface

Rip or scarify along the contour (never up-and-down, which creates drainage lines), respread stripped topsoil, and leave a roughened, stepped surface that catches seed, water and mulch.

d. Kill the weeds now

Pre-plant weed control is the most under-rated step — weeds outcompete natives and are far harder to remove later. Where appropriate, use a registered herbicide and follow the label (check actives, rates and withholding on Spray Hub).

e. Armour steeper slopes with matting

On steep, high-risk or concentrated-flow slopes, install a biodegradable erosion-control blanket to hold soil and seed until plants take over. Match product to slope:

ProductSuits slope up toLastsBest for
Jute mesh / soil-saver~3:1 (open weave) to 2:1~6–18 monthsGentle–moderate slopes establishing in one season; budget large areas; pre-slit for tubestock.
Coir blanket 400 gsm~2:1, moderate flow2–3 yearsWetter sites; batters needing more than one season.

💡 Installing matting (the bits people skip)

Dig a ~150 mm anchor trench across the top and key the blanket in; roll downslope with full soil contact (air gaps = failure); overlap 100–150 mm and shingle the upslope roll on top so water flows over joins; pin ~1/m², closing to every 300 mm on edges/overlaps (150 mm pins in most soils, 300 mm in sand). Coir logs pegged on the contour add check-points on long slopes.

Step 3 — Choose the right plants

Good erosion plantings are layered communities, not one species. Five principles:

  1. Local provenance — species indigenous to your area, ideally from local seed; adapted and won't become weeds.
  2. Layer the community — groundcovers + tussock/strappy plants & native grasses + shrubs + trees.
  3. Speed + depth — quick coverers for the first season, deep-rooted anchors for the long term.
  4. Match the microsite — moisture, drainage, aspect, salt and shade; a wet creek toe is not a hot dry crest.
  5. Never introduce a weed — avoid vigorous exotics (and some non-local natives); check your state weed list.

✅ Broadly reliable "workhorse" natives

Lomandra longifolia (spiny-headed mat-rush), Dianella (flax-lilies), Themeda triandra (kangaroo grass), Ficinia nodosa (knobby club-rush), Carpobrotus (pigface, coastal), Myoporum parvifolium (creeping boobialla), plus Acacia (wattles) and Casuarina/Allocasuarina (she-oaks) for structure. Always confirm the locally-indigenous species for your area.

⚠️ Two things to avoid

Vetiver grass is effective but not native — skip it if you specifically want natives. And avoid "cheap and fast" exotics (kikuyu, exotic ivy/creepers) that become serious environmental weeds. Cross-check anything unfamiliar against your state/territory weed list before planting.

Step 4 — Plant it effectively

Timing — plant into moisture

Plant after rain, into moist soil. Best windows across most of Australia: autumn (roughly Feb–April) ahead of winter rain, and spring after frost risk. In the seasonal tropics, plant into the early wet season. Avoid young or actively-growing stock in cold winter or summer heat.

Method — match to scale

Small–medium: tubestock

Advanced seedlings are the most reliable — plant through pre-slit jute where used.

Large areas: hydroseeding

Seed, mulch, binder (± fertiliser) sprayed on for fast cover over big or inaccessible slopes.

Broadscale: direct seeding

Native grass & forb seed drilled/broadcast; cheap at scale but slower and weather-dependent.

Steep / creek: matting + tubestock

Coir blanket for instant armour, planted through with strappy binders and shrubs.

Layout & density

Plant along the contour in staggered/offset rows so no continuous up-and-down gap channels water. Go dense — close spacing gives quick cover, shades out weeds and creates a sheltered micro-climate. Layer groundcovers and grasses between and beneath shrubs and trees.

LayerTypical spacing / density for erosion work
Groundcovers & tussock grasses / strappy plantsDense — commonly 4–8 plants/m² (~30–50 cm apart) for fast closed cover; sedges on wet toes clumped closer.
Shrubs~1–2 m apart (open shrubland ≈ 500–1,000 plants/ha).
Trees (canopy)3–10 m apart by target community (closed forest ~1,500/ha; open forest ~500–1,000/ha; woodland ~250–500/ha).

🎯 The one number that matters

All this density has a single aim: reach ≥70% ground cover as fast as possible — the threshold where erosion drops away.

Planting technique

  1. Dig a hole twice the tube width; tease out circling roots.
  2. Plant at soil level, backfill and firm in to remove air pockets.
  3. Form a small basin/lip on the downhill side to catch water.
  4. Water in immediately — even in wet weather — to settle soil around roots.
  5. Mulch around (not against) the stem.

Step 5 — Establish & maintain

The first 1–3 years decide whether the slope is fixed for good; most failures happen here, from neglect.

Mulch — 75–100 mm

Coarse organic mulch (or the matting itself), kept off stems; avoid fine mulch that washes on steep slopes.

Water — first summer

Deep, infrequent watering through establishment beats frequent sprinkles; wean off as plants take.

Weeds — the #1 job

Relentless weed control for 2–3 years is the biggest predictor of success.

Protect

Tree guards shelter tubestock from wind, frost and rabbits; control grazing until established.

⚠️ Go easy on fertiliser — especially phosphorus

Australian natives evolved on low-nutrient soils; many — particularly Proteaceae (Banksia, Grevillea, Hakea) — are highly phosphorus-sensitive and can be killed by standard fertilisers. Use a low-P native-specific fertiliser or none, and build soil structure instead.

Monitor & top up

  • Set up photo-points and re-shoot the same angles each season to track cover and spot rills early.
  • Infill and replace failed plants promptly — gaps are where erosion restarts.
  • Check matting and pins after big storms; re-pin lifted edges.
  • Once cover exceeds ~70% and plants are self-supporting, the matting has done its job and breaks down naturally.
  • Only take on the area you can realistically maintain.

Common mistakes that undo the job

  • Ignoring water from above — the fastest way to lose a new planting.
  • Planting too sparsely — slow cover means weeds win and soil keeps moving.
  • Exposing dispersive subsoil and leaving it bare — invites rilling and tunnel erosion.
  • Skipping pre-plant weed control — the single most common cause of failure.
  • Planting in the wrong season without follow-up water.
  • Using weedy exotics for "quick cover" — a new problem that spreads off-site.
  • Poor matting install — no anchor trench, air gaps, or reversed overlaps.
  • Fertilising natives (especially with phosphorus) and killing P-sensitive species.
  • No maintenance — walking away after planting.

✅ Best-practice checklist

Divert upslope water → batter/bench to ≤2:1 → treat dispersive soil with gypsum → rip on the contour → control weeds → match matting to steepness → choose local layered natives → plant dense on the contour → into moisture, watered & mulched → weed, water and monitor for 2–3 years.

Legal & safety

  • Native vegetation & clearing rules — clearing existing native vegetation (even to "tidy up" a slope) is regulated in every state/territory and may need approval. Check with your council or state environment/planning body before removing vegetation.
  • Waterways & riparian works — works on or near creeks, drainage lines and banks may require a permit (state water/environment authority). Riparian zones have their own rules and often their own funding/support.
  • Weed hygiene — clean machinery, boots and tools between sites to avoid spreading weed seed and pathogens (e.g. Phytophthora). Source clean, local-provenance stock.
  • Chemical use & records — only use herbicides registered (or permitted) for the situation, follow the label, and keep the spray records your state requires for commercial application.
  • Working safely on slopes — steep batters are a fall and machinery-rollover hazard. Plan access, work top-down where possible, and never stand directly below loosened soil or operating machinery.

Plants, seed & where to source them

Match sourcing to method and always favour local provenance.

  • Tubestock & groundcovers — local native, Landcare/community and council revegetation nurseries (best for local-provenance stock).
  • Native seed & seed mixes — specialist suppliers such as Australian Seed (australianseed.com), which stocks native grasses, Lomandra, Juncus and Baumea, groundcovers, saltbush (Atriplex) and bluebush (Maireana) for harsh sites, wattles (Acacia), and offers seed pre-treatment (including smoke) to lift germination. Ideal for hydroseeding and direct seeding at scale.
  • Advice & local species lists — your regional Landcare group, catchment/NRM body, Greening Australia, Local Land Services and council revegetation guides can confirm the right indigenous species and provenance for your exact area.
  • Chemicals for pre-plant weed control — check actives, rates and withholding periods on Spray Hub and always follow the label.

Frequently asked questions

What are the best native plants for erosion control on a slope?

Fibrous-rooted strappy natives and native grasses do most of the soil-binding — Lomandra longifolia, Dianella, Themeda triandra, Poa and Microlaena. Add fast groundcovers (Myoporum parvifolium, Carpobrotus, Kennedia) for instant surface cover, plus Acacia and Casuarina/Allocasuarina for deeper anchoring. Always choose species indigenous to your area.

How steep can a slope be before I need erosion matting?

Up to about 3:1 you can usually plant directly with dense cover and mulch. At 2:1 and steeper, install matting to hold soil and seed — jute for gentler/short-term sites, coir for steep/wet/slower sites. Around 1:1 use heavy 700 gsm coir and consider engineered support.

When is the best time to plant a slope in Australia?

Plant into moist soil after rain. Best windows: autumn (roughly Feb–April) ahead of winter rain, and spring after frost. In the seasonal tropics, plant into the early wet season. Avoid mid-summer heat and cold winters for young stock.

How do I stop runoff washing my new plants away?

Deal with water from above first — divert upslope runoff with a contour bank, catch drain or diversion drain to a stable vegetated outlet before planting. Then plant along the contour and use matting on steeper sections so flow never cuts a rill through new plants.

What is dispersive (sodic) soil and why does it matter?

Dispersive soils fall apart in water — an air-dry crumb dropped into clear water throws a milky halo. They're prone to rilling and tunnel erosion, especially if fresh subsoil is exposed and left bare. Treat with gypsum and organic matter, and cover quickly with mulch, matting and plants.

How densely should I plant?

Densely — commonly 4–8 groundcovers or tussock grasses per square metre — so cover closes fast and weeds are shaded out. The target is at least 70% living and dead ground cover as quickly as possible.

Should I fertilise native plants on a slope?

Go easy. Many natives — especially Proteaceae (Banksia, Grevillea, Hakea) — are highly phosphorus-sensitive and can be killed by standard fertilisers. Use a low-P native-specific fertiliser or none, and focus on soil structure and organic matter.

Jute or coir matting — which should I use?

Jute suits gentle-to-moderate slopes (about 3:1 to 2:1) establishing within one season and lasts ~6–18 months. Coir lasts longer and handles steeper, wetter sites: 400 gsm for ~2:1 (2–3 years) and 700 gsm for steep 1:1 high-rainfall or creek-bank sites (3–5 years).
